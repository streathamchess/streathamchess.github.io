### Rapid Play Tournament completes our 150th Anniversary Celebrations

Our 150th Anniversary celebrations came to a magnificent conclusion in a 40-strong Rapid on Saturday 17th September 2022 at the Mark Bennett Streatham Centre. It was played over six hard-fought rounds at 25 minutes plus 5 seconds per minute. There was a strong contingent of 16 from S&BCC, with another 16 or so other clubs represented. We were also delighted to welcome two juniors from Ukraine: Sasha Andronova, who has already been playing for us in the Croydon League, and Bohdan Terler of Maidenhead CC.

{% include image.html url="/assets/image_uploads/nl23-rapid-organisers.jpg" description="James Toon (far left), the editor (middle) and Richard Tillett (right)" %}

S&BCC member James Toon (far left in pic) was Tournament Controller for the day. He was super-efficient in keeping things to a tight schedule (thanks, James!), aided and abetted by Richard Tillett (right) and Paul Ritchie from the club Management Team – with your editor (middle) trying to keep up.

We were delighted that Bell Ribeiro-Addy MP for Streatham was able to open the tournament. We presented her with a copy of *Movers and Takers* (our club history – if you’ve not yet got your copy we hope to put it online soon), and she reciprocated by generously sponsoring a prize. Thanks Bell!

They both fought through without losing a game, each winning a £100 first prize. ‘Well done’ to them both. Runners-up were Richard Cannon (UNATS), and Gokhan Kizilay (Lewisham) in the Major and Minor respectively (£50 each). Grading prizes of £40 went to Zafar Iqbal (Kings Head/S&BCC), and Alex Spencer (S&BCC).

### Library Group flourishing – Richard Tillett reports

We endured a couple of chilly weeks in November when the Library’s central heating was broken (it’s been fixed now), but numbers have been good, with typically 20 or so chessers and their parents/carers at peak time (usually around 6.00-6.30).

There’s a regular core group and most weeks there’s a newcomer or two checking us out. In addition, there’s a much larger number of once-in-a-while folks who we don’t see so often. It’s always a pleasure to welcome back people who last came in those distant days before Covid landed.

Visitors to London stop by from time to time, and we even had a grandmaster from Spain join our sessions for a couple of weeks to play some blitz games with us. I don’t know if anyone took a game off him, but he certainly made short work of me.

**We’ll be open as usual during the Christmas and New Year period except for Tuesday 27 December when the Library will be closed.**

### Club Annual General Meeting 7th October 2022 – Richard Tillett (Club Secretary), and Paul Ritchie (Treasurer) report

This was our first AGM since OTB chess resumed and it was a relief to get back to something resembling business as usual. For the 2022/23 season we decided to increase our presence in the two central London leagues and the Croydon League, while trimming back our representation in the Surrey League because of difficulties getting players out for away matches.

Our finances are in good shape. We posted a small surplus for the 2021-22 season despite lower player turnout (due to the lingering impact of covid) and extra expenditure to support our 150th anniversary events. Our healthy financial performance was due largely to the fantastic support of our membership base who gave generous donations plus a number of players paying full subscriptions when they only played a handful of games.

The AGM decided to keep subscription rates at same level for 2022/23 as in the previous season: £45 for full membership; £22.50 for concessionary membership (including juniors), and a £4.50 game fee. We’ve been able to maintain subscription levels at the same level for five years and we remain one of the lowest cost clubs in London.

Chess clubs depend on members helping with the considerable volume of admin. More assistance would be greatly appreciated, particularly with keeping the club website up-to-date. If you think you might be able to help, please drop me an email or call me (contact details are in the PDF newsletter).

### POTYs

The annual Player Of The Year (POTY) award has been running since 2008/9. The POTY is a member who has done sterling service that season for the club: in the front line at the board (OTB and/or Online), and possibly also behind the scenes as a captain or organiser. The club management team consults team captains before making the award at the AGM.

{% include image.html url="/assets/image_uploads/nl23-john-hodgson-poty.jpg" description="John Hodgson (right) receives the Player of the Year trophy" %}

Last season (2020-21) it was awarded to John Hodgson for his excellent results (8.5/14) in the OL4NCL (playing on boards 1 or 2 for the first team), stepping up also to take over the reins of our re-emerging team in the London League. John lives in Essex, and your Keeper of the Trophy had to wait until the Anniversary Rapid to grab a chance to make a formal presentation (see photo).

Belated congratulations to John!

#### Antony Hall: U2000 Central London Congress, Imperial College, November

At the beginning of November, I played in the well-established three-day Central London Congress at Imperial College. Generous move rate (90m + 30spm); comfortable playing conditions; ECF and FIDE-rated; over-18s only (due to licensing restrictions): what’s not to like? ### False Moves

The scandal of (alleged) cheating in GM OTB chess has made it to the national press. Whether the publicity is good or bad for the game, time will tell. Scandals in cricket (ball-tampering, body-line bowling), football (the hand of God, shirt-pulling), boxing (too numerous to list), etc, haven’t done any lasting damage: it’s gamesmanship. By contrast, drug-based performance enhancement in athletics is an existential threat akin to computer assistance in OTB chess. The latter is a necessarily modern phenomenon (quaintly, when Deep Blue beat Gazza in 1967 his allegation was that the computer had improper assistance from a human), and – for OTB anyway – antidotes will surely be found.

Leaving intentional cheating aside, look here at some examples of rule-breaking in serious chess, all be it accidental. The first one appeared in a local club game a few years back. It may be an example of something more common than we all realise: the illegal move double oversight. It was reported at the time on the club website here: [https://streathamchess.org/2016/01/21/martin.html](https://streathamchess.org/2016/01/21/martin.html) My opponent was the genial Nick Keene of Wimbledon CC who sadly died suddenly in June 2022.

### And who is Rodney Barking, I hear you ask

To satisfy our curiosity Mr Barking kindly provided the potted chess biography below. We asked him for a selfie, but he was too shy to oblige. Could this be him?

*Rodney Dylan Dreadnought Randall Barking, aka Rodney Barking, discovered chess at the age of 10 when his grandfather gave him a chessboard and £1 to buy the pieces. He taught himself to play from ‘Chess for Children’ Raymond Bott and Stanley Morrison [both members of S&BCC in the 1960s – Ed]. At school in Leicestershire he was a big fish in a small chess pond, and he dreamed of becoming world champion [Didn’t we all – Ed]. Reality intervened at the county junior championships when he finished some way behind contemporaries Mark Hebden and Glenn Flear. Undaunted he took on his first captaincy at the age of 15 with the Loughborough 3rd team, and soon he was captain of an Oxford University team. On moving to London, he joined the nearest chess club: Streatham & Brixton [Aha! – Ed]*

*Rodney’s appetite for the game knew no bounds. When he joined the Civil Service, he revived the dormant Home Office Chess Club, playing in the Civil Service League and the London Commercial League. For the Civil Service Chess Association he volunteered for, and did, anything: from adjudicator (though later he disliked adjudication on principle) to General Secretary. Now believing that running everything was his duty and calling, he came to the attention of the medical profession. He was diagnosed with Eton Syndrome.*

*More recently, Rodney founded the Central London League, and the London Public Service League. [And is active again in S&BCC – Ed] His latest venture is online correspondence chess and this year he has started over 100 games. He was honoured to represent his country in international friendlies against Mexico and the Philippines. He is currently playing in the British Championships Candidates Tournament.*

Thanks Rodney, we know you a bit better now; but please don’t overindulge again on Eton Mess.

### Game of the Century – but which century was it?

**Robin Haldane – James Toon, Civil Service League, 1996**  
C44: Scotch, Goering Gambit

{% include lichess_pgn.html study="zTGNuSvW" chapter="a7J4Eu3n" %}

Welcome to the 19th Century…

{% include image.html align="left" url="/assets/image_uploads/nl23-after-ng4.png" description="After 9….Ng4" %}

**1.e4 e5 2. Nf3 Nc6 3.d4 exd4 4.c3 dxc3 5.Bc4 cxb2 6.Bxb2** *(The Danish Gambit. In true Haldane style, Robin sacrifices two pawns for a lead in development, open lines and an attack.)* **6...d6** *(6...Bb4+ 7.Nc3 Nf6 is a decent alternative. Black will castle, play …d6 and start defending.)* **7. Qb3 Qd7 8.Bc3 Nh6 9.Na3 Ng4** *(It's all about e5. I was planning …f6 and …Nge5 to mount a barricade and stop White pushing the pawn.)* **10.O-O-O?** *(What? This move offers the exchange and a pawn, just to remove one of Black's defenders. Can it be sound? I couldn't work it out at the board, so decided not to take on f2. However…)* **10...f6** *(Meant to prevent e5, but White plays it anyway. Instead 10...Nxf2 11.e5 Nxd1 12.Rxd1 d5 13.Bxd5 Bc5 and Black keeps the extra material and survives.)*

**11.e5 fxe5 12.Rhe1 Qe7 13.Rd5 g6?** *(There is no time for this. 13...Bd7 planning to castle long was the safer course here.)* **14.h3 Bh6+** *(I was planning to retreat but White keeps sacrificing material on e5 until eventually he wins my queen: 14...Nf6 15. Nxe5 dxe5 16.Rdxe5 Nxe5 17.Rxe5.)* **15.Kb2 Be6?** *(I can't remember why I played this. White gets two pieces for a rook and the attack continues.)* **16.hxg4 Bxd5 17.Bxd5 Bg7** *(Black is one move away from consolidating. Unfortunately that's one move too far… )* **18.Qxb7**

{% include image.html url="/assets/image_uploads/nl23-after-qxb7.png" description="After 18 Qxb7" %}

**18...Rb8** *(White's queen is pinned – but at what cost?)* **19.Bxc6+ Kf7 20. Bd5+ Kf6** *(Retreating to the back rank breaks the connection between the rooks and allows Qxb8+.)* **21.g5+ Kf5 22.g4+ Kxg4** *(Robin sensed (correctly) that checkmate was now on and spent most of his remaining time looking for the killer blow.)*

{% include image.html align="left" url="/assets/image_uploads/nl23-killer-blow.png" description="Looking for the killer blow" %}

**23.Re4+?** *(This wasn't it. Instead the game should finish 23.Rg1+ Kh5 24.Be4 Rxb7+ 25.Ka1 I have a material advantage of queen, rook and three pawns for three minor pieces, but it's not enough. 25...Qxg5 26.Rxg5+ Kh6 27.Bd2 Bf8 28.Rxg6+ Kh5 29.Rg5+ Kh6 30.Rg4+ Kh5 31.Rh4#)* **23...Kf5 24.Bd2 Rxb7+ 25.Kc2** *(Quite rightly not capturing on b7. The bishop is needed on d5 where it stops the black king escaping via e6.)* **25...Qxg5** *(Giving up my queen is the only way to avoid mate.)* **26. Bxg5?** *(As soon becomes apparent, Robin should have captured on g5 with the knight rather than the bishop.)* **26...c6 27. Bxc6 Rc7 28.Rc4 Rhc8 29.Bd7+ Rxd7 30.Rxc8** *(All this was forced. Now Black gets a piece back. This wouldn't work with the knight rather than the bishop on g5.)* **30...e4 31. Nd2 Kxg5 32.Nxe4+ Kf4 33.Nd2 d5 34.Kd3 h5** *(I decided my best plan was to push the h-pawn and try to tie down some of White's pieces.)*

**35.Ke2 h4 36.Kf1 h3 37.Kg1 g5 38.Nc2 g4 39.Ne1 Kf5 40.Nd3 Bh6** *(Here, stronger would have been 40...Rb7 41.Nf1 Rb1 42.Rc1 Rxc1 43.Nxc1 d4 when a draw is the most likely outcome.)* **41.Nf1 Bg7 42.Ng3+ Kg5 43.Rc6 Rb7** *(Now White should maintain the pressure with Rc5 and win eventually.
